03 · Document pages 6–8 · Physiology

What Happens to the Brain in Meditation?

The document next moves from broad benefits to the brain, stress regulation and measurable body activity. Open each + for more detail.

01Brain activityAttention, emotional regulation and changes reported across different brain regions.

Meditation does not simply “switch off” the brain. Different styles of practice can change how attention, sensory information and emotions are processed across several connected regions.

The course describes meditation as bringing intuitive and analytical ways of thinking into greater balance. Research findings vary by technique, experience level and study design, so brain activity should be described as changing rather than universally increasing or decreasing.

02Prefrontal and frontal cortexPlanning, reasoning, attention and the experience of mental rest.

The prefrontal cortex helps us pause, plan, evaluate choices and direct attention. The material connects meditation with emotional balance and with a greater ability to recover after difficult events.

It also describes mental rest in parts of the frontal cortex during meditation. These statements are not contradictory: different frontal subregions can show different activity patterns, and results depend on the kind and stage of meditation.

03Parietal lobe, thalamus and reticular formationSensory processing, orientation, alertness and relaxation.

Parietal lobe: helps organize sensory information and our sense of the body in time and space. Reduced processing may contribute to feeling less aware of time or physical boundaries.

Thalamus: acts as a major relay and filter for sensory signals entering the brain. During inward-focused practice, attention to outside input may lessen.

Reticular formation: helps regulate alertness and readiness to respond. Lower arousal during meditation can support a feeling of deep relaxation. Claims about extra-sensory perception in the course belong to its spiritual framework rather than established neuroscience.

04Autonomic nervous systemSympathetic fight-or-flight activity and parasympathetic rest-and-recover activity.

The sympathetic branch prepares the body to respond quickly to danger or pressure. That response is useful in a genuine emergency, but repeated activation during ordinary conflicts can contribute to tension and stress.

The parasympathetic branch supports rest, digestion and recovery. A calm meditation practice may help some people move toward this quieter state and notice a reaction before acting on it; it does not eliminate the body's necessary stress response.

05Metabolic rateRespiration, heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen consumption and muscle relaxation.

During quiet meditation, breathing and heart rate may slow, muscle tension may ease and oxygen use or blood pressure may change as the body settles. The course describes this as a wakeful, lower-metabolic state rather than sleep.

The size of these changes varies across people and practices. Specific figures in the course - including claims about oxygen consumption, blood lactate or intelligence - require reliable research citations before being presented as established facts.

This part contains testable physiological claims. Strong numerical or causal statements should be paired with reliable research sources before publication or presentation.

05 · Document pages 10–13 · Traditional Model

Science of Seven Bodies

The course describes increasingly subtle layers of experience. These are presented as a spiritual framework rather than established anatomical structures. Open each + for the document-aligned explanation.

01Annamaya Kosha · physical or food body

Anna means food. This is the gross physical body, sustained by nourishment and experienced in time and space. The course connects this layer with becoming aware of the body's inner sensations during meditation and with practices such as Hatha Yoga and Karma Yoga.

02Pranamaya Kosha · vital-energy or etheric body

Prana means vital energy. Within this framework, this sheath animates the physical body and is associated with breath, movement, assimilation, excretion and reproduction. The material links awareness of this layer with feelings of lightness or freedom from the usual sense of physical weight.

03Manomaya Kosha · mental or astral body

Manas means mind. This layer processes thoughts, emotions and sensory impressions and is described as directing the physical body through prana. The course presents it as the field of changing desires, pleasure, pain, doubt and illusion, and says meditation helps us observe and move inward through it.

04Vijnanamaya Kosha · knowledge or causal body

Vijnana means knowing. This is described as the sheath of intellect, discernment, decision, will and the sense of “I.” In the course model, meditation helps soften ego-identification and gives access to deeper wisdom; Raja Yoga and Jnana Yoga are associated with this level.

05Anandamaya Kosha · bliss or spiritual body

Ananda means bliss. The document describes this as a subtle layer close to the Self, characterized by peace, joy, love and rest rather than an ordinary emotion. It is associated with self-realization, while also being presented as a sheath that a seeker ultimately moves beyond.

06Vishwamaya Kosha · cosmic body

This layer is described as cosmic oneness: movement from ego toward non-ego and from “I” toward an inclusive sense of “we.” The course uses the image of a drop losing its separate identity and discovering itself as the ocean.

07Nirvanamaya Kosha · nirvanic body

The seventh layer is described as an uncaused happening rather than something reached by a method. In this spiritual model, even the sense of cosmos falls away into total stillness, emptiness or non-being - an experience the document says cannot be adequately communicated in words.
