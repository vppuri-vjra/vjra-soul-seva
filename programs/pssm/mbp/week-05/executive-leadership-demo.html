Executive Leadership · New Product Development

From Reaction to Adaptation

Using the thinking–feeling cycle and neuroplasticity to build more adaptive leadership responses.

A focused four-minute teaching demonstration for leaders developing and scaling new products under uncertainty.

4-Minute Demo · Executive Leadership Team

Audience: Executive leadership team

Application: Developing and scaling a new product

Mind-Jogging Opening

Good morning, everyone.

Imagine that your team has invested six months in a new product, and the latest customer test has failed.

You have five seconds: what is your first response?

Do you ask who was responsible—or what the team has learned?

That choice reveals the thinking–feeling cycle. Today, we will examine how this cycle shapes leadership behaviour and how neuroplasticity can help us change it.

Teaching script

Developing a new product involves uncertainty. Customer needs may change, prototypes may fail, costs may rise and results may arrive later than expected.

In these moments, leaders do not respond only to facts. We also respond through an ongoing cycle of thinking and feeling.

Cycle — Thinking and Feeling, and Feeling and Thinking

Imagine that a prototype fails. A leader may think, “We are falling behind.” That thought produces concern or anxiety in the body. The feeling then influences the next thought: “The team is not capable,” or “We must launch immediately.”

Experience → Thought → Feeling → Reaction → Decision → Outcome

When this pattern is repeated, it can become automatic. The leader may begin responding to every setback with pressure, blame, excessive control or rushed decisions.

This affects the product team. People become cautious, stop sharing problems and avoid experimentation. Innovation declines precisely when the organization needs it most.

Neuroplasticity — Building a Different Response

Neuroplasticity is the brain’s ability to strengthen, weaken and reorganize connections through experience and repeated practice. In leadership terms, habitual responses are learned—but they are not necessarily permanent.

A leader can interrupt the old cycle by creating a short pause between the event and the response. Consider the failed prototype again. Instead of reacting immediately, pause, observe the breath and ask:

  • What are the facts?
  • What am I feeling or assuming?
  • What response will best support learning and organizational purpose?

The response can then change from “Who is responsible for this failure?” to “What has this prototype taught us, and what should we test next?”

One different response does not create lasting change. Repeatedly choosing curiosity over blame, reflection over impulse and learning over defensiveness helps establish a new leadership pattern.

Challenge → Pause → Awareness → Clear thinking → Constructive action → Learning

Key message: Thoughts and feelings influence leadership decisions. Repeated reactions become habits, while repeated conscious responses build adaptability.

In new product development, this helps leaders create a culture in which teams can learn, experiment and innovate without being controlled by old reactions.

Thank you.
