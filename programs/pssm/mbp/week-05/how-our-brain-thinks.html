How Our Brain Thinks

The brain uses substantial energy, especially for concentration, problem-solving, and decision-making. To operate efficiently, it combines slower conscious processing with faster automatic processes that handle familiar tasks, learned behaviours, habits, and protective responses.

Thinking and Feeling, and Feeling and Thinking

Simple summary

  • Thoughts influence feelings in the body.
  • Those bodily feelings influence the thoughts that follow.
  • Together, thoughts and feelings create our present state of being—the working relationship between mind and body.
  • When the same thought–feeling cycle repeats, it can become an automatic emotional habit.
  • Awareness and deliberate new responses can interrupt the old loop and help create a different state of being.
ConceptWhat happensPractical meaning
Energy conservationFocused reasoning and difficult decisions require effort, so the brain develops efficient routines.Repeated actions can become quicker and require less conscious attention.
Conscious processingSupports deliberate attention, evaluation, problem-solving, and new choices.Useful when learning, changing a habit, or responding to an unfamiliar situation.
Automatic processingSupports familiar routines, learned habits, basic regulation, and rapid protective responses.Efficient, but old patterns may continue without being consciously examined.
Thought–feeling loopThoughts influence bodily feelings; those feelings can then influence the next thoughts.Repeated loops can create a familiar emotional state and reinforce habitual responses.
Learning and conditioningRepeated neural and bodily responses strengthen familiar patterns.A person may begin reacting automatically from past experience rather than present circumstances.
Creating changeAwareness makes it possible to interrupt an old loop and practise a different thought, feeling, or action.Consistent new choices support neuroplastic change and gradually establish new habits.

Thinking and feeling reinforce one another

  • A thought can initiate neural and chemical activity associated with a bodily feeling.
  • The brain receives information from the body, and this can influence the next thought.
  • When the cycle repeats, the brain and body learn the pattern together.
  • Familiar emotions can become cues for familiar thoughts.
  • Deliberate awareness helps interrupt the familiar cycle and create a different response.
How Science Works

Course model: cellular response to a chemical signal

PhaseStepSummary
Phase 1 · Signal Reception & InitiationMolecule of Emotion (Neuropeptide)The process starts with a chemical messenger—a neuropeptide—carrying an electromagnetic “bar code” generated by thoughts and feelings.
Cell Receptor SiteThis messenger acts like a key finding its exact lock, docking into a specific receptor site on the cell membrane.
Signal TransductionOnce docked, the chemical message is received and passed into the cell.
Phase 2 · Genetic Activation & Protein SynthesisDNA Opens & UnwindsThe incoming message reaches the cell nucleus, causing the DNA to open and unwind so the corresponding gene can be read.
Gene Expression & New Protein FormationThe cell reads the message from the DNA and synthesizes a new protein, such as a specific hormone.
Release Into BodyThe newly created hormone or protein is released through the cell membrane and into the body, reinforcing the physical feeling that started the loop.

Course model: subconscious body conditioning

A real-world example: the chronic stress loop

StageWhat may happenOpportunity for change
1 · The triggerA past conflict produced a strong stress response involving hormones such as cortisol and adrenaline.Recognize that a present event may be activating a memory or learned threat response.
2 · The learned patternRepeatedly replaying the event can strengthen associated thoughts, bodily sensations, attention patterns, and reactions.Notice the familiar bodily signals without automatically treating them as evidence of current danger.
3 · The automated presentEven in a safer setting, the person may scan for problems and interpret ambiguity through the old stress pattern.Pause, examine present facts, regulate the stress response, and choose where to direct attention.
4 · Repeating—or changing—the futureChoices and relationships may repeat yesterday’s emotional habits when past feelings continue to guide today’s reactions.Practise a different interpretation and response repeatedly so a new pattern can become more accessible.

Science note: This diagram is a simplified teaching model. Actual relationships among thoughts, emotions, neurotransmitters, hormones, receptors, gene regulation, health, and ageing involve many interacting systems. A thought does not directly activate one specific gene or hormone through a single universal pathway.
