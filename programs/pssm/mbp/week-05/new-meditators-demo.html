New Meditators · First Session · 10 Minutes

Pause, Observe and Choose

Three minutes of Ānāpānasati followed by an introduction to the thinking–feeling cycle, four thought patterns and neuroplasticity.

Teachable Point of View:

  • Thoughts and feelings continually influence one another.
  • Awareness creates space to choose what happens next.
  • Repeated constructive responses can develop more adaptive patterns.

Audience: General public and first-time meditators

1 · Begin2 · Meditate3 · Observe4 · Recognize5 · Practise6 · Choose

01 · Interactive Opening · 0:00–1:00

One Event, Different Responses

Facilitator: “Imagine that you send an important message. Several hours pass, but there is no reply. What is the first thought that enters your mind?”

  • “They must be busy.”
  • “Maybe they are upset with me.”
  • “Something must be wrong.”
  • “I will wait and check later.”

“The event is the same, but different thoughts can create different feelings and reactions. This is the thinking–feeling–thinking cycle.”

“Before exploring the cycle, let us observe our own minds through a short meditation.”

02 · Ānāpānasati Meditation · 1:00–4:00

Observe the Natural Breath

“Please sit comfortably. Keep your back naturally upright, without making the body stiff. Place your hands comfortably on your lap and gently close your eyes.”

“Bring your attention to your natural breath. There is no need to breathe deeply or change the breath. Simply observe the breath as it enters and leaves.”

“Breathing in, know that you are breathing in. Breathing out, know that you are breathing out.”

“Thoughts may appear. This is natural. You do not need to stop, follow or judge them. Notice the thought gently and return your attention to the breath.”

Pause silently for approximately 90 seconds.

“Now become aware of your body sitting here. Notice the sounds around you. When you are ready, gently open your eyes.”

03 · Thinking–Feeling Cycle · 4:00–5:30

Notice What Happens Next

“During meditation, you may have thought, ‘Am I doing this correctly?’, ‘My mind is too busy’, or ‘I feel calm.’ Each thought may have produced a different feeling.”

Situation → Thought → Feeling → Next Thought → Response

Example: Someone has not replied. “They are ignoring me” may create hurt and an angry response. “I do not yet know why” may create balance and a calmer response.

“The first thought often appears automatically. Awareness gives us an opportunity to examine what comes next.”

04 · Four Thought Patterns · 5:30–7:30

Which Pattern Appears?

Disastrous Thought

“Everything will go wrong, and I will not be able to handle it.” It assumes the worst before we know the facts.

Negative Thought

“This is difficult. I probably cannot do it.” It focuses mainly on limitations and obstacles.

Positive Thought

“This is difficult, but I can take one useful step.” It recognizes difficulty while remaining open to improvement.

Miraculous Thought

“There may be possibilities I cannot see yet.” It joins a larger possibility with conscious action.

Audience activity: “You are asked to do something important that you have never done before. Which thought appears first—A, B, C or D?” Invite participants to show one, two, three or four fingers. “There is no judgement; we are simply recognizing a present pattern.”

“These are patterns of thought—not fixed labels for people.”

05 · Neuroplasticity · 7:30–9:15

Practise a New Pattern

Neuroplasticity is the brain’s capacity to change and strengthen connections through experience, learning and repeated practice.

“One different thought does not instantly transform the brain. Change develops through repetition. When we repeatedly pause, examine a thought and choose a constructive response, we practise a different pattern.”

Pause → Notice → Name → Choose → Repeat
  • Pause: Do not react immediately.
  • Notice: What am I thinking and feeling?
  • Name: Which pattern is present?
  • Choose: What is a more balanced next thought?
  • Repeat: Practise when the pattern returns.

“Meditation supports this process by helping us notice thoughts without immediately being controlled by them.”

06 · Reflection & Close · 9:15–10:00

What Will You Strengthen?

“Meditation does not require us to have no thoughts. It teaches us to observe thoughts, return to the present and choose what we wish to strengthen.”

Closing question: “Which thought pattern would I like to notice—and what new response would I like to practise?”

Key Takeaways

  • Thoughts and feelings continually influence one another.
  • The first thought may be automatic, but the next response can become conscious.
  • Thought patterns are not our permanent identity.
  • Awareness and repeated practice can help the brain develop more constructive patterns.
